`# Part 1 — The Model`

[The nine Enneagram types follow from a few simpler facts about people. People of each type tell themselves a consistent story about who they are, and each story leaves out something they usually cannot see about themselves.

Everything a person deals with belongs either to the physical world of objects, actions and consequences, or to the inner world of thought, feeling and meaning. In the physical world, a person's mistake is corrected from outside, whatever the person believes. In the inner world, whatever a person thinks or feels becomes part of what the person is thinking or feeling about.

A person can be turned toward either world in one of three ways: drawn toward what attracts, alert to what could go wrong, or stepped back, neither reaching for what attracts nor trying to avoid what threatens. Whichever of the three ways a person holds, the person is good at something, overlooks something, and cannot easily bear something. The way a person holds toward the physical world and the way the person holds toward the inner world can differ. A type's qualities come partly from what each of a person's two ways is good at. A type's qualities also come from what happens when a person carries the principles of one world into the other world, where the principles do not hold.]

`## Chapter 1: A New Theory of the Enneagram`

> Nine is a strange number for a typology to arrive at. It is not two, or three, or four, where schemes of temperament usually settle.

**What it previews:** in the order of Part 1's chapters: why the types are nine, the nine as people with their blind spots, the two worlds and how a mistake behaves in each, the three ways of being turned toward a world, and how two ways together give a type its qualities.

`## Chapter 1: A New Theory of the Enneagram`

[Three things, one in each of two places, make exactly nine combinations. The count comes to nine because the two places are genuinely different. In a person, the three things are three ways of being turned toward whatever the person is dealing with, and the two places are the physical world and the inner world. Toward each world, one of the three ways hardens into habit. A person's strengths, weaknesses and fears, and how the person characteristically goes wrong, all follow from which way has hardened toward each world. Each of the nine types has two hardened ways, one toward each world, where a type in the classical Enneagram has one.]

> Nine is a strange number for a typology to arrive at. It is not two, or three, or four, where schemes of temperament usually settle.

**What it previews:** in the chapter's order: the counting (three things in two places give nine, and only because the places differ), what the three and the two are in a person, the hardening into habit and what follows from it, and how this differs from the classical Enneagram.
