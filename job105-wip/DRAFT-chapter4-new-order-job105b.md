## Chapter 4: The Three Orientations

Only the same three orientations are possible toward the content of each realm. This book abbreviates them **A** (enthusiasm), ~~**B**(caution),~~ [**B** (caution),] and ~~**Z**(detachment).~~ [**Z** (detachment).]

### Attraction, aversion, want and fear

Attraction and aversion are the two directions in which a person can be drawn: toward something, or away from it. Some attractions and aversions are only felt. Liking and disliking are of this kind. They are not chosen, and they need not motivate the person to do anything.

A want is what motivates a person to bring something about: to have something, to keep it, or to be rid of it. An attraction can raise a want to have something or to keep it. An aversion can raise a want to be rid of something.

A fear is an aversion to something that might happen, such as losing a job or spilling a cup of coffee. A fear can arise from an attraction, as the fear of losing someone arises from being drawn to them. A fear can also arise from an aversion, as the fear that a pain will return arises from disliking the pain. A fear motivates the person to keep the feared thing from happening. Once a fear is conceived, the prospect is present in the person's mind, so a fear works like any other aversion.

~~Some attractions and aversions also involve the will: craving and coveting on the side of attraction, hatred on the side of aversion.~~ A fear raised in a thought has a want behind it, the want the thought was about. Behind the fear of losing a job is the want to keep the job. Some fears are instinctual. A situation sets an instinctual fear off directly, with no want behind it. Instinctual fears are uncommon in ordinary adult life and common in infancy.

[Some attractions and aversions also involve the will: craving and coveting on the side of attraction, hatred on the side of aversion.]

### Taking in and noticing

Everything that happens in a realm is taken in, whole and at once, with nothing assembled and nothing inferred. Picking one part out of what was taken in is a second and different act. This book calls that act noticing, and it happens automatically.

What has been taken in stays out of view until ~~a person wants~~ something in ~~it.~~ [it bears on a want or a fear.] That is when the noticing happens. So motivation is what brings the noticing on. Motivation also sets how much of what was taken in is open to noticing. The taking in itself is not limited. A want or a fear pulls a person's noticing toward what the want or fear is about. The harder a want or a fear pulls, the less of anything else is open to noticing.

Taking in goes on all the time, whatever a person is doing. What changes from moment to moment is the noticing. Sometimes a want or a fear pulls a person's noticing toward one thing. At other times nothing is pulling, and the whole realm is open to noticing. A person moves between noticing one thing and having the whole realm open many times a day. What a person notices most is determined by the orientation they hold.

### Intuition

Intuition is a person noticing something now that was taken in earlier and never noticed at the time. A person can remember something happening only if they noticed it at the time. So the person has no memory of the thing happening. Not everything that is taken in and not noticed is lost. Some of it remains, but not as concepts that can be put into words. The person knows the thing and cannot say how they know.

[What intuition reaches need not be old. Something taken in a moment ago is reached the same way as something taken in decades ago. Intuition is also the only route to what a person learned before having words for anything, since none of what was learned then was ever in words.]

### Turning away from a fear

[A fear can be looked at and settled, or turned away from.] A fear that was not settled is raised again later, and motivates the person again. Sometimes a fear grows until the fear is more than the person can bear, and panic triggers an automatic reaction that turns the person away from the fear. From then on, the person turns away automatically from fears about the same matter. This automatic turning away is denial. Denial happens before a later fear is noticed. Denial is something a person does, but not consciously. To decide not to look at something, a person would first have to notice it. Denial happens before the noticing, so there is nothing yet for the person to decide about.

Denial can become very firm. When someone else points the thing out, the person in denial turns away from it again. [Where the person would notice the thing, there is a blank.] The blank may be replaced by a less threatening thought. Denial covers only the thing a person is in denial about. Everything else in the person's life can be noticed in the ordinary way.

Turning away eases the anxiety for the moment. Neither the anxiety nor the easing is noticed. Both are taken in, and the easing ~~adds to the panic holding~~ [is what holds] the turning away in place. What the person feels afterwards is unease, with nothing to explain it. What a person has turned away from cannot be found by deliberately looking for it, because even trying to conceptualize it would [probably] set off the anxiety again. But the thing can come back into view by itself, at a time when nothing sets off the anxiety. And intuition can act on it, since only the person's awareness of it is blocked.

### Being caught, and what a person can bear

Each orientation comes with something a person cannot easily bear. How much pain that produces depends on how strongly a person holds the orientation. The more strongly a person holds the orientation, the more deeply caught the person is. Deeply caught, a small ~~disappointment~~ [trouble] produces a great ~~turning away.~~ [deal of pain.] Less caught, the same ~~disappointment~~ [trouble] produces less. [The more pain a trouble produces, the more likely the person is to turn away from it.]

What a person can bear is set by what previous thoughts and events, even unnoticed ~~ones~~ [ones,] have laid down. Stress is whatever lowers a person's capacity to bear pain. It can come from anywhere: a bad night, an illness, a worry carried in from somewhere else. A person under stress can bear less, so the same thing would be harder to face than it was the day before.

Stress does not stay in the realm it came from. A person has one capacity to bear pain, not one for each realm, so a stressful week in the physical world leaves less to face an inner fear with. What differs between a person's two realms is how much pain the same kind of event produces in each. Stress lowers the one capacity, so stress lowers what a person can face in both realms at once.

One thing follows from that. Being deeply caught in a realm is stressful. So a person deeply caught in one realm can bear less in both realms. The two realms are therefore not independent, whichever one a person is more deeply caught in.

### Enthusiastic, cautious and detached

**A, enthusiastic.** Drawn toward what attracts, focused on possibility and gain, playing down risk. An enthusiastic person wants one particular thing and is acting on it, so what is open to their noticing is narrow. When something goes wrong — a blocked goal, a complication, an irritation — an enthusiastic person has no native answer to a negative at all, and does the one thing attraction can do: turns toward the next positive. The blocked goal is neither worked through nor fled. The person simply stops noticing the blocked goal. What an enthusiastic person cannot easily bear is failing to get rewarded.

An enthusiastic person's competence is **doing**: wholehearted engagement with the realm — action in the physical, immersion in the psychic. Doing can be loud or quiet. Chasing a goal is doing in its active style; hours lost in a garden or a piece of music is doing in its quiet one. Speed and volume are matters of temperament, and a slow, still person can be as thoroughly enthusiastic as a fast, loud one.

**B, cautious.** Alert to threat, to loss, to what could go wrong. A cautious person fears several outcomes at once, and working out how a whole situation will go is what those fears require, so what is open to their noticing is wider. When something goes wrong, a cautious person engages, and the trouble gets looked at, analyzed and managed. What a cautious person cannot easily bear is losing control.

A cautious person's competence is **understanding**: a worked-out grasp of how things work, what could fail, and how events will unfold. Understanding is arrived at deliberately — noticing a thing, generalizing from it, testing the generalization against what happens next, and revising it. In some people this shows up as explicit plans and contingencies; in others it is held silently, a map kept current with no schedule attached. Both are the same competence. What marks it is wanting to comprehend a situation before trusting it.

**Z, detached.** Stepped back, neither reaching for what attracts nor trying to avoid what threatens. A detached person still ~~dislikes discomfort.~~ [minds being disturbed.] A detached person's own want is peace: to be free of the pull of wanting and fearing. While nothing is disturbing the person, wanting peace pulls the noticing nowhere. A detached person does not look for disturbances. When something goes wrong, a detached person fixes it as efficiently as possible, [or] steps back and lets the difficulty go, or does not look at it in the first place. What a detached person cannot easily bear is the discomfort that fear leaves behind.

A detached person's competence is **being able to notice more**. While nothing is disturbing a detached person, nothing pulls their noticing toward anything in particular, so the whole realm stays open to their noticing. An enthusiastic or a cautious person is being pulled by a want or a fear, so less of the realm is open to their noticing.

Detachment has two faces. The two faces differ in what happens when a disturbance arrives, such as the discomfort a fear leaves behind. When a disturbance is noticed, what a detached person wants is to quiet the disturbance, and their noticing goes to that. Sometimes a detached person looks at the disturbance and wants to solve what is disturbing them. Then a great deal stays open to their noticing, and the noticing can be deep. This is the widest of all. Sometimes the person simply wants the disturbance to go away. Then very little stays open to their noticing. This is the narrowest of all, narrower than an enthusiastic person's.

Detachment lies between caution and enthusiasm. [The two faces are the two ends of detachment: looking at a disturbance is the caution end, and wanting the disturbance to go away is the enthusiasm end.] When a fear arrives for a detached person, the fear comes to the caution end first, where fears are still noticed. The fear then moves on toward the enthusiasm end, where a person wants the fear to go away. The more attention a person gives the caution end, the more likely a fear is to be settled. At the enthusiasm end, a person is more likely to turn away from anything negative. When panic turns a person away from a fear, the panic happens at the caution end, where the fear is still in view, and the turning away is completed at the enthusiasm end.

Each competence is real. The enthusiast really does start things and really does throw themselves in. The cautious one really does see what is coming. The detached one really can notice anything in the realm, while nothing is disturbing them. Each competence is a genuine capacity that people holding the other two orientations do not have. Each competence is also part of why a person keeps the orientation they hold.

What separates the three orientations is what a person holding each one notices. Each person largely overlooks what people holding the other two orientations notice. The difference is not one of subject matter: two people can face the same trouble with the same facts in front of them, one noticing what could still be gained and the other what could still go wrong. Noticing is something a person does, again and again, and a person notices something only when it matters to them: when it bears on something they want or fear.
