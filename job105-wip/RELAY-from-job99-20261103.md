You may be right that “every” claims too much. The book’s own definition in Chapter 4 points to the split:

* A fear that arises from an attraction clearly has a want behind it. The fear of losing a job comes from the want to keep the job, and the fear of losing someone comes from being drawn to them. Riso’s basic fears are of this kind, and so are many ordinary fears.
* A fear that arises from an aversion has a want behind it only in a thin sense. The fear that a pain will return comes from disliking the pain. The “want” behind it is just the dislike turned around: the want to be rid of the pain. The book’s definition of a want includes “to be rid of it,” so the claim stays true, but it adds nothing.
* Some fears seem to come before any want. A startle at a snake or at a sudden drop is felt before anything is wanted.
